# Doors and windows

What the book gives us (own words; line numbers are in the owner's text export, and nothing here is quoted):

* **Doorways, not doors.** A hall's "doors" are the passages between halls: openings with frames and jambs (the chalk marks that visitors make are low on the doorframe or jamb), wide enough that an
  albatross could pass through with its wings out (lines 94, 419, 851, 1883, 1969). The text never gives them leaves, hinges, handles, locks or any way to close, and never says that anything
  opens, shuts, creaks or slams. The one hall with "a single door" and no windows is dark because it has one opening and no light, and a draught comes out of it (847, 867). A door that opens
  and closes appears only in the other world (2554, 2730).
* **Windows are tall openings onto the courtyards** (102, 112), and in a hall's far wall or in a corridor (867, 881). The weather and sea reach them: the lower halls' windows look grey-green and
  spattered with foam, the upper halls' grey-white with cloud and lit by lightning in rows (106, 108); stars "blaze down through" them (849). The only hint of glass is the sky reflected in some,
  across a courtyard (409), which a wet stone or a still pool would give as well. No glass, panes, shutters, bars or curtains in the House. (A smashed window and a lowered blind are the other
  world's, 2189, 2730.)

Rules (canon W-015, W-016):

1. A door is an opening. It can be wide, high, framed, blocked by rubble (the derelict halls), half under water, or hung with a seaweed line; it has no leaf.
2. A window is an open aperture. Light, air, rain, spray, birds and the stars all come in; nothing on them can rattle, crack, fog or reflect a room.
3. No sound or sentence may use a fitting that does not exist: no hinge, latch, lock, creak, slam, rattle of a door or window, panes, shutters or glass. `tests/fittings.test.ts` scans the
   player-facing source for those words.
4. "Door" as a word for the opening is the book's own (it capitalises Door and Doorway alike), so the narrator and the parser keep saying doors, and "a door opens in the east wall" means that
   it leads onward.

Audited and changed (the architecture flourishes and a few lines of narration were our own inventions that broke the rules):
* a pair of iron-bound doors, "shut", in the architecture list became a doorway of the same size with iron rings in its jambs; a "pair of tall doors, closed" became a pair of tall doorways;
  a "small plain door" became a small doorway;
* the rose window with its coloured glass became one with stone tracery that has fallen away, throwing a lattice of light; "panes of light" and "bars of the windows" became slabs of light and mullions;
  the lamps' glass became bronze;
* "the roof timbers creak" in a storm (the House has no timber) became "the broken vault groans"; "someone left this door open" (a muttered line) became "someone has been through this doorway";
* comments that put rain "on the windows" or "against the glass", or a hall heard "through one open door", now say through and doorway;
* the wind's door-and-window rattle was built and removed (design/wind.md).

Left as they are, on purpose:
* the carved door-shapes (frames, panels, handles and hinges in the solid stone, that lead nowhere): stone pictures of doors, like the statues, and they say so;
* sluice gates of timber and iron in the water flourishes: machinery for water, not a door or window, but our own invention; the owner may want them out, since the book mentions no machinery;
* statue subjects that include doors, glass slippers and the like (figures list): carved scenes.
